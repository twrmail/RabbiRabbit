Opening

Written by AIImagine hearing that someone gave up what they treasured most for people who had given them nothing back. That is the heart of these three verses. In a few short lines, Jesus tells us why God sent his Son, what the Son came to do, and what happens to the one who trusts him. These verses are not about a God hunting for reasons to condemn us. They show a God whose love moves first, and they say plainly what is at stake in how we answer. If you are new to following Jesus, this is a good place to stand and look around.

John 3:16

Wonder at a love for the worthless Written by AI
Henry marvels that the great, holy God would love a worthless, wicked world. This love was not kept for one nation. Christ came in love for the whole world, outsiders as well as insiders.
Matthew Henry in his own words
Behold, and wonder, that the great God should love such a worthless world! That the holy God should love such a wicked world with a love of good will, when he could not look upon it with any complacency. This was a time of love indeed, Eze 16:6,8. The Jews vainly conceited that the Messiah should be sent only in love to their nation, and to advance them upon the ruins of their neighbours; but Christ tells them that he came in love to the whole world, Gentiles as well as Jews, 1Jo 2:2.

A gift beyond what people would do Written by AI
Chrysostom hears Jesus saying: do not be shocked that I must be lifted up. The Father gave his Son for ungrateful slaves. Few would do that even for a friend, so every word here carries weight.
John Chrysostom in his own words
What He saith, is of this kind: Marvel not that I am to be lifted up that ye may be saved, for this seemeth good to the Father, and He hath so loved you as to give His Son for slaves, and ungrateful slaves. Yet a man would not do this even for a friend, nor readily even for a righteous man; as Paul has declared when he said, "Scarcely for a righteous man will one die." Now he spoke at greater length, as speaking to believers, but here Christ speaks concisely, because His discourse was directed to Nicodemus, but still in a more significant manner, for each word had much significance.

The physician who comes to heal Written by AI
Augustine pictures Christ as a physician who comes to heal the sick. Whoever ignores the doctor's orders harms himself. Christ comes as Saviour, not judge. If you are not saved by him, you will be judged by yourself.
Augustine in his own words
"For God sent not His Son into the world to judge the world, but that the world through Him may be saved." So far, then, as it lies in the physician, He is come to heal the sick. He that will not observe the orders of the physician destroys himself. He is come a Saviour to the world: why is he called the Saviour of the world, but that He is come to save the world, not to judge the world? Thou wilt not be saved by Him; thou shalt be judged of thyself And why do I say, "shall be judged"?
Written by AIIf Jesus came to save and not to condemn, what do we make of judgment? The next verse does not dodge the question. It sets two ways of living side by side and shows what turns on one thing: whether we believe in the Son.

John 3:18

He who believes in him is not judged. He who doesn’t believe has been judged already, because he has not believed in the name of the only born Son of God.
Matthew Henry · Matthew Henry's Commentary on the Whole Bible
Not condemned, but acquitted Written by AI
Henry says the believer, though a sinner who stands guilty by his own confession, is not condemned. The process is stopped. This is more than a reprieve: he is acquitted and discharged, not dealt with by strict justice for what his sins deserve.
Matthew Henry in his own words
[3.] From all this is inferred the happiness of true believers: He that believeth on him is not condemned, Joh 3:18. Though he has been a sinner, a great sinner, and stands convicted (habes confilentem reum—by his own confession), yet, upon his believing, process is stayed, judgment is arrested, and he is not condemned. This denotes more than a reprieve; he is not condemned, that is, he is acquitted; he stand upon his deliverance (as we say), and if he be not condemned he is discharged; ou krinetai—he is not judged, not dealt with in strict justice, according to the desert of his sins.

🕍 Rabbi Road

Returning by way of Romans 5:8:

But God commends his own love toward us, in that while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.

(chosen by code: the top cross-reference for this passage at OpenBible.info, 995 votes)

Written by AILet us take a short walk to this verse in Romans and then return. Our passage tells us that God so loved the world. Romans tells us when that love acted: while we were yet sinners. God did not wait for the world to clean itself up and then send his Son. Henry says God commends his love the way a merchant commends his goods, in the most winning and endearing way. This love also does not leave us as it found us, for Christ died to save us from our sins, not in them. Now go back to our passage. The world in the first verse is not a world of the deserving. The salvation in the second is offered to people who are still sinners. And the not condemned of the last rests on what Christ has already done, not on our record. Believing is receiving what was given while we were still far from ready.
Matthew Henry · Matthew Henry's Commentary on the Whole Bible · Romans 5:8
Commendeth his love, as merchants commend their goods when they would put them off. This commending of his love was in order to the shedding abroad of his love in our hearts by the Holy Ghost. He evinces his love in the most winning, affecting, endearing way imaginable. While we were yet sinners, implying that we were not to be always sinners, there should be a change wrought; for he died to save us, not in our sins, but from our sins; but we were yet sinners when he died for us.
