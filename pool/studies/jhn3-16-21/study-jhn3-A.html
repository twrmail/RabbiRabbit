John 3:16

For God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life.

Rabbit's question: How far does God's love go, and why does it surprise us?

Jamieson, Fausset & Brown · Overview · Commentary Critical and Explanatory on the Whole Bible
Modern language, by AI
Jamieson, Fausset and Brown ask which words of the gospel preachers and missionaries have spoken most often in every age, which have stirred millions of people, and which have brought so many to the feet of Christ. They point to these words about God's love, so simple and yet so majestic, as the ones that kindle self-giving love in cold and selfish hearts.
Jamieson, Fausset & Brown in his own words
16. For God so loved, &c.—What proclamation of the Gospel has been so oft on the lips of missionaries and preachers in every age since it was first uttered? What has sent such thrilling sensations through millions of mankind? What has been honored to bring such multitudes to the feet of Christ? What to kindle in the cold and selfish breasts of mortals the fires of self-sacrificing love to mankind, as these words of transparent simplicity, yet overpowering majesty?
Matthew Henry · What it means · Matthew Henry's Commentary on the Whole Bible
Modern language, by AI
Matthew Henry invites us to be amazed that the great and holy God would love a worthless and wicked world with a love of good will, even though he could take no pleasure in what the world had become. He adds that some Jews wrongly thought the Messiah came only in love for their own nation, but Christ says he came in love for the whole world, Gentiles as well as Jews.
Matthew Henry in his own words
Behold, and wonder, that the great God should love such a worthless world! That the holy God should love such a wicked world with a love of good will, when he could not look upon it with any complacency. This was a time of love indeed, Eze 16:6,8. The Jews vainly conceited that the Messiah should be sent only in love to their nation, and to advance them upon the ruins of their neighbours; but Christ tells them that he came in love to the whole world, Gentiles as well as Jews, 1Jo 2:2.
John Chrysostom · What it means · Homily on the Gospel of John 27
Modern language, by AI
Chrysostom hears Jesus saying: do not be shocked that I must be lifted up so that you may be saved. This pleases the Father, who loved you enough to give his Son for slaves, and ungrateful ones at that. Chrysostom notes that a person would hardly do this even for a friend, or readily for a good person. He adds that Jesus speaks briefly here, yet every word carries weight.
John Chrysostom in his own words
What He saith, is of this kind: Marvel not that I am to be lifted up that ye may be saved, for this seemeth good to the Father, and He hath so loved you as to give His Son for slaves, and ungrateful slaves. Yet a man would not do this even for a friend, nor readily even for a righteous man; as Paul has declared when he said, "Scarcely for a righteous man will one die." Now he spoke at greater length, as speaking to believers, but here Christ speaks concisely, because His discourse was directed to Nicodemus, but still in a more significant manner, for each word had much significance.
John Calvin · The teaching · Calvin commentary JHN
Modern language, by AI
John Calvin says John does not claim that God acted because he saw something in us worth saving. The credit for our rescue goes entirely to God's love. Because God gave his Son so that people would not perish, Calvin concludes that until Christ steps in to rescue the lost, everyone is headed for eternal destruction.
John Calvin in his own words
Nor does he say that God was moved to deliver us, because he perceived in us something that was worthy of so excellent a blessing, but ascribes the glory of our deliverance entirely to his love. And this is still more clear from what follows; for he adds, that God gave his Son to men, that they may not perish. Hence it follows that, until Christ bestow his aid in rescuing the lost, all are destined to eternal destruction.

John 3:17

For God didn’t send his Son into the world to judge the world, but that the world should be saved through him.

Rabbit's question: Did the Son come to judge or to save?

Matthew Henry · What it means · Matthew Henry's Commentary on the Whole Bible
Modern language, by AI
Matthew Henry says God's design in sending his Son was that the world might be saved through him. He came with salvation in view and in hand, so the offer of life and salvation is sincere and will be made good to everyone who accepts it by faith. God sent him into a guilty, rebellious world, not as a passing visitor like the angels sent before, but as one who lives here.
Matthew Henry in his own words
Secondly, Here is God's design in sending hi Son into the world: it was that the world through him might be saved. He came into the world with salvation in his eye, with salvation in his hand. Therefore the aforementioned offer of live and salvation is sincere, and shall be made good to all that by faith accept it (Joh 3:17): God sent his Son into the world, this guilty, rebellious, apostate world; sent him as his agent or ambassador, not as sometimes he had sent angels into the world as visitants, but as resident.
Augustine · What it means · Tractates on John 12 (this note covers 17-18)
Modern language, by AI
Augustine says that, as far as it is up to the physician, Christ came to heal the sick, and a patient who will not follow the physician's orders destroys himself. Christ is called the Saviour of the world because he came to save the world, not to judge it; the one who refuses to be saved by him is judged by himself.
Augustine in his own words
"For God sent not His Son into the world to judge the world, but that the world through Him may be saved." So far, then, as it lies in the physician, He is come to heal the sick. He that will not observe the orders of the physician destroys himself. He is come a Saviour to the world: why is he called the Saviour of the world, but that He is come to save the world, not to judge the world? Thou wilt not be saved by Him; thou shalt be judged of thyself And why do I say, "shall be judged"?
John Calvin · The word · Calvin commentary JHN
Modern language, by AI
John Calvin says John repeats the word world so that no one who keeps to the road of faith will think himself shut out. Calvin reads judge here as condemn, and says this shows the real purpose of Christ's coming: there was no need for him to come to destroy people who were already ruined.
John Calvin in his own words
The word world is again repeated, that no man may think himself wholly excluded, if he only keep the road of faith. The word judge ( πρίνω ) is here put for condemn, as in many other passages. When he declares that he did not come to condemn the world , he thus points out the actual design of his coming; for what need was there that Christ should come to destroy us who were utterly ruined?
John Chrysostom · The teaching · Homily on the Gospel of John 28 (this note covers 17-18)
Modern language, by AI
Chrysostom says Christ has two comings with different purposes. The first came to forgive, not to examine our actions; the second will be to examine, not to forgive. So of the first Jesus says he came not to condemn the world but to save it, while at the second he will set the sheep on his right hand and the goats on his left.
John Chrysostom in his own words
For there are two Advents of Christ, that which has been, and that which is to be; and the two are not for the same purpose; the first came to pass not that He might search into our actions, but that He might remit; the object of the second will be not to remit, but to enquire. Therefore of the first He saith, "I came not to condemn the world, but to save the world"; but of the second, "When the Son shall have come in the glory of His Father, He shall set the sheep on His right hand, and the goats on His left."

John 3:18

He who believes in him is not judged. He who doesn’t believe has been judged already, because he has not believed in the name of the only born Son of God.

Rabbit's question: What happens to the person who believes?

Matthew Henry · The teaching · Matthew Henry's Commentary on the Whole Bible
Modern language, by AI
Matthew Henry draws out the happiness of believers. Although a person has been a great sinner and stands convicted, once he believes the process is stopped and judgment is held back. This is more than a delay: he is acquitted and discharged, and is not dealt with by the strict justice his sins deserve.
Matthew Henry in his own words
[3.] From all this is inferred the happiness of true believers: He that believeth on him is not condemned, Joh 3:18. Though he has been a sinner, a great sinner, and stands convicted (habes confilentem reum—by his own confession), yet, upon his believing, process is stayed, judgment is arrested, and he is not condemned. This denotes more than a reprieve; he is not condemned, that is, he is acquitted; he stand upon his deliverance (as we say), and if he be not condemned he is discharged; ou krinetai—he is not judged, not dealt with in strict justice, according to the desert of his sins.
Jamieson, Fausset & Brown · The word · Commentary Critical and Explanatory on the Whole Bible
Modern language, by AI
Jamieson, Fausset and Brown say the believer has passed from death to life the moment he believes. The one who is condemned already is the one who rejects the single way out that God gave his Son to provide, and so chooses to stay condemned.
Jamieson, Fausset & Brown in his own words
18. is not condemned—Having, immediately on his believing, "passed from death unto life" (Joh 5:24).
condemned already—Rejecting the one way of deliverance from that "condemnation" which God gave His Son to remove, and so wilfully remaining condemned.
John Chrysostom · Living it · Homily on the Gospel of John 28
Modern language, by AI
Chrysostom stresses that the one not judged is the one who believes, not the one who is merely curious or nosy. He adds that people who claim to know God but deny him by their works are not true believers at all, and that Jesus means such a person is not judged in this one respect: for his works he will suffer a severer punishment, but having once believed, he is not punished for unbelief.
John Chrysostom in his own words
"He that believeth on the Son, is not judged." He that "believeth," not he that is over-curious: he that "believeth," not the busybody. But what if his life be unclean, and his deeds evil? It is of such as these especially that Paul declares, that they are not true believers at all: "They profess that they know God, but in works they deny Him." But here Christ saith, that such an one is not "judged" in this one particular; for his works indeed he shall suffer a severer punishment, but having believed once, he is not chastised for unbelief.
