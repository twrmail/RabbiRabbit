Study ET0: Loved, Sent, Believed

Scripture and public-domain commentary, with a narrative written by AI.

Some verses are so familiar that we stop hearing them. These three verses tell us what God felt, what God did, and what it means for anyone who believes. Read them slowly, with four old teachers beside you.

A Love That Gives

John 3:16For God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life.
The passage begins with love, and Matthew Henry will not let us hurry past it. He wants us to stop and wonder.
Matthew Henry · Matthew Henry's Commentary on the Whole Bible
Behold, and wonder, that the great God should love such a worthless world! That the holy God should love such a wicked world with a love of good will, when he could not look upon it with any complacency. …
Read the whole passage from Matthew Henry
Behold, and wonder, that the great God should love such a worthless world! That the holy God should love such a wicked world with a love of good will, when he could not look upon it with any complacency. This was a time of love indeed, Eze 16:6,8. The Jews vainly conceited that the Messiah should be sent only in love to their nation, and to advance them upon the ruins of their neighbours; but Christ tells them that he came in love to the whole world, Gentiles as well as Jews, 1Jo 2:2.
Albert Barnes asks what kind of love this is. God does not approve of sin, but he still wants good for sinners.
Albert Barnes · Notes on the New Testament
… This does not mean that God approved the conduct of men, but that he had benevolent feelings toward them, or was earnestly desirous of their happiness. God hates wickedness, but he still desires the happiness of those who are sinful. He hates the sin, but loves the sinner. …
Read the whole passage from Albert Barnes
Verse 16. For God so loved. This does not mean that God approved the conduct of men, but that he had benevolent feelings toward them, or was earnestly desirous of their happiness. God hates wickedness, but he still desires the happiness of those who are sinful. He hates the sin, but loves the sinner. A parent may love his child and desire his welfare, and yet be strongly opposed to the conduct of that child. When we approve the conduct of another, this is the love of complacency; when we desire simply their happiness, this is the love of benevolence.
And Calvin reminds us that nothing in us earned it.
John Calvin · Calvin commentary JHN
Nor does he say that God was moved to deliver us, because he perceived in us something that was worthy of so excellent a blessing, but ascribes the glory of our deliverance entirely to his love. And this is still more clear from what follows; for he adds, that God gave his Son to men, that they may not perish. …

And this is still more clear from what follows; for he adds, that God gave his Son to men, that they may not perish. Hence it follows that, until Christ bestow his aid in rescuing the lost, all are destined to eternal destruction.

Sent to Heal

John 3:17For God didn’t send his Son into the world to judge the world, but that the world should be saved through him.
If God sent his Son, why did he send him? These verses tell us: not to judge, but to save. Augustine pictures a doctor at the bedside.
Augustine · Tractates on John 12
"For God sent not His Son into the world to judge the world, but that the world through Him may be saved." So far, then, as it lies in the physician, He is come to heal the sick. He that will not observe the orders of the physician destroys himself. He is come a Saviour to the world: why is he called the Saviour of the world, but that He is come to save the world, not to judge the world? …

He that will not observe the orders of the physician destroys himself. He is come a Saviour to the world: why is he called the Saviour of the world, but that He is come to save the world, not to judge the world? Thou wilt not be saved by Him; thou shalt be judged of thyself And why do I say, "shall be judged"?
Matthew Henry hears the question every guilty heart asks when heaven comes near: is it peace?
Matthew Henry · Matthew Henry's Commentary on the Whole Bible
Ever since man sinned, he has dreaded the approach and appearance of any special messenger from heaven, as being conscious of guilt and looking for judgment: We shall surely die, for we have seen God. If therefore the Son of God himself come, we are concerned to enquire on what errand he comes: Is it peace? Or, as they asked Samuel trembling, Comest thou peaceably? And this scripture returns the answer, Peaceably.

Believing and Not Believing

John 3:18He who believes in him is not judged. He who doesn’t believe has been judged already, because he has not believed in the name of the only born Son of God.
The last verse draws the line. Jamieson, Fausset and Brown put the two sides plainly.
Jamieson, Fausset & Brown · Commentary Critical and Explanatory on the Whole Bible
18. is not condemned—Having, immediately on his believing, "passed from death unto life" (Joh 5:24).
condemned already—Rejecting the one way of deliverance from that "condemnation" which God gave His Son to remove, and so wilfully remaining condemned.
So the door is open, and the gift is already given. Believing is how we walk through.

🕍 Rabbi Road

Returning by way of Romans 5:8:

But God commends his own love toward us, in that while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.

(chosen by code: the top cross-reference for this passage at OpenBible.info, 995 votes)

Romans 5:8 makes the same point from another side: God showed his love while we were still sinners. Henry marks how great that love is. Coming back to John 3:16, we see that the world God loved was not a good world waiting to be rewarded.
Matthew Henry · Matthew Henry's Commentary on the Whole Bible · Romans 5:8
Some think he alludes to a common distinction the Jews had of their people into ndyqym—righteous, hsdym—merciful (compare Isa 17:1), and rssym—wicked. Now herein God commended his love, not only proved or evidenced his love (he might have done that at a cheaper rate), but magnified it and made it illustrious. This circumstance did greatly magnify and advance his love, not only put it past dispute, but rendered it the object of the greatest wonder and admiration: "Now my creatures shall see that I love them, I will give them such an instance of it as shall be without parallel."
